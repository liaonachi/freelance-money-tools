## Why "salary ÷ hours" doesn't work

If you've ever tried to set your freelance rate by taking your old salary and dividing it by 2,080 (40 hours × 52 weeks), you've probably ended up quoting a number that leaves you broke by October. That math works for an employee. It doesn't work for a freelancer, for three reasons:

1. **Not all your hours are billable.** Between finding clients, sending invoices, answering emails, and learning new tools, most freelancers only bill 20–30 hours out of a 40-hour week.
2. **You now pay for things your employer used to cover.** Self-employment tax, health insurance, retirement contributions, software, equipment — it all comes out of what you charge.
3. **You don't get paid time off.** Every week you take off is a week of zero income, unless you build it into your rate ahead of time.

Once you account for all three, the gap between "salary ÷ hours" and a sustainable freelance rate is usually much bigger than people expect — often 1.5–2x.

## The formula, step by step

Our [hourly rate calculator](/tools/hourly-rate) uses four inputs to work backward from the income you actually want to keep:

**1. Billable hours per year**

```
billableHours = billableHoursPerWeek × (52 − weeksOffPerYear)
```

Start with how many hours per week you can realistically bill — not how many hours you work, how many you can put on an invoice. Most full-time freelancers land between 20 and 30. Then subtract the weeks you plan to take off (vacation, holidays, and slow periods where no client work comes in).

**2. Gross revenue needed**

```
grossNeeded = (targetIncome + annualExpenses) / (1 − taxSetAsidePct/100)
```

Add your target take-home income to your annual business expenses (software, equipment, insurance, a portion of your rent if you work from home, and so on). Then "gross up" that number by your tax set-aside percentage, because the money you set aside for taxes has to come from your invoices too — it's not a cost that disappears, it's a cost you have to bill for. If you're not sure what percentage to use, our [tax set-aside calculator](/tools/tax-set-aside) will estimate it from your actual federal and state brackets.

**3. Hourly and day rate**

```
hourlyRate = grossNeeded / billableHours
dayRate = hourlyRate × 8
```

Divide the gross revenue you need by your billable hours, and you have your hourly rate. Multiply by 8 for a day rate, which is the most common way freelance work is quoted in the US market.

## A worked example

Let's say you want to take home $80,000 a year, you can realistically bill 25 hours a week, you're planning on 6 weeks off, your annual business expenses run about $8,000, and you want to set aside 25% for taxes.

- Billable hours: 25 × (52 − 6) = **1,150 hours/year**
- Gross needed: ($80,000 + $8,000) / (1 − 0.25) = **$117,333**
- Hourly rate: $117,333 / 1,150 ≈ **$102/hour**
- Day rate: $102 × 8 ≈ **$816/day**

Notice that $102/hour is nearly double a naive $80,000 / 2,080 hours ≈ $38/hour calculation. That's the real cost of self-employment showing up in the math — not a markup, just the actual bill for taxes, benefits, and unbillable time that used to be invisible on a pay stub.

## Common mistakes freelancers make

**Overestimating billable hours.** If you're new to freelancing or your client pipeline isn't steady yet, 40 hours a week of billable work is not realistic — plan for 15–20 hours until you have a full calendar.

**Forgetting slow-growth costs.** Annual expenses should include more than software subscriptions: professional development, a laptop replacement fund, and — if you want one — retirement contributions like a SEP-IRA. Freelancers don't get an employer match, so if you want to save for retirement, it has to be built into your rate.

**Guessing at the tax percentage.** A flat 25% works as a rough starting point, but your actual number depends on your income level, state, and whether you're accounting for self-employment tax on top of income tax. Run your numbers through the [tax set-aside calculator](/tools/tax-set-aside) to get a more accurate figure, then come back and recalculate your rate.

**Never revisiting the number.** Your rate should change as your expenses, income goals, or billable capacity change — not just once a year. Rerun the calculation whenever something shifts.

## FAQ

**Why is my freelance rate so much higher than my old salary ÷ hours?**
Because it has to cover the hours you can't bill (finding clients, admin, learning), plus the taxes and benefits an employer used to pay for, and paid time off you no longer get automatically. None of that shows up in a simple salary-to-hourly conversion.

**How many billable hours per week is realistic?**
Most full-time freelancers land between 20 and 30 billable hours a week. If you're new or your client pipeline is inconsistent, use a more conservative number — 15 to 20 hours is a safer starting point.

**Should I include retirement savings in my annual expenses?**
Yes. Freelancers don't get an employer 401(k) match, so if retirement saving matters to you, treat your contribution target (for example, toward a SEP-IRA) as a cost of doing business and add it to your annual expenses.
