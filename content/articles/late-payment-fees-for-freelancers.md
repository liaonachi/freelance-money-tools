## Why you need a late fee clause at all

Late payments are one of the most common frustrations freelancers deal with — surveys of freelance workers consistently put "getting paid late" near the top of the list of business problems, right behind finding clients in the first place. A late fee clause doesn't just compensate you for the delay; it changes the incentive for the client. Net-30 terms with no consequence for missing them are, in practice, optional. A stated late fee makes the due date real.

The good news: writing one into your contract is simple, and calculating what's owed when a client actually pays late is even simpler with the [invoice late fee calculator](/tools/late-fee).

## What to put in your contract

A late fee clause needs three things to be enforceable and clear:

1. **A specific rate**, stated as a percentage per month or per year (not a vague "reasonable interest").
2. **A start date**, usually the day after the invoice due date — not the invoice date itself.
3. **Whether it compounds**, and almost every freelance contract should say "simple interest" — meaning the fee is calculated on the original invoice amount only, not on interest that has already accrued. Compounding interest brings additional legal complexity that most freelance contracts don't need.

A typical clause reads something like: *"Invoices unpaid 30 days after the due date accrue a late fee of 1.5% per month (18% per year), simple interest, calculated on the outstanding balance."*

## Common rates — and their limits

**1–1.5% per month** (roughly 12–18% annualized) is the range most freelance and small-business contract templates use in the US. It's high enough to matter to a client, but low enough to be a normal, defensible business term rather than something that looks punitive.

Before you write a number into a contract, check two things:

- **Does your state cap interest rates on unpaid invoices?** Usury laws vary significantly by state, and some states set a maximum rate for contracts that don't specify one, and a separate (often lower) maximum for what you're allowed to charge even if you do specify a rate. This isn't something a calculator can check for you — look up your state's current rules or ask a lawyer if a client is disputing a large fee.
- **Is the rate actually in the signed contract?** A late fee you never agreed on in writing is much harder to collect. If your existing contracts don't have a clause, add one going forward — it doesn't help retroactively.

## How the math works

Once a payment is late, the calculation is simple interest, accrued daily:

```
dailyRate = monthlyRatePct / 100 / 30      (or annualRatePct / 100 / 365)
interest = invoiceAmount × dailyRate × daysLate
total = invoiceAmount + interest + flatFee
```

Monthly rates are converted to a daily rate using a 30-day month, which is the standard approximation used in most contract templates — it avoids the complication of invoices landing in different-length months. Annual rates use 365 days.

### A worked example

Say you sent a $2,500 invoice with a 1.5%-per-month late fee, and the client pays 30 days after the due date, with no additional flat fee.

- Daily rate: 1.5 / 100 / 30 = 0.0005 (0.05% per day)
- Interest: $2,500 × 0.0005 × 30 = **$37.50**
- Total owed: $2,500 + $37.50 = **$2,537.50**
- Effective annual rate: 0.05% × 365 ≈ **18.25%**

That last number — the effective annual rate — is useful for sanity-checking your own contract terms against any rate caps you look up, since caps are usually expressed as an annual percentage even if your contract states a monthly one.

If you also charge a flat late fee (some freelancers add a flat $25–50 admin fee on top of interest), that amount gets added to the total separately — it doesn't compound with the interest calculation.

## Sending the late fee to a client

When you actually invoice for a late fee, it helps to show your work: the original amount, the days late, the rate you're applying (with a reference to the contract clause), and the resulting total. Clients are much more likely to pay without pushback when the number is transparent and traceable to something they signed.

## FAQ

**What late fee rate should I put in my contract?**
1–1.5% per month (about 12–18% annualized) is a common range for freelance and small-business invoices, but check whether your state caps late fee or interest rates before you set one — usury limits vary by state.

**Can I charge both a flat fee and interest?**
Yes, as long as your contract clearly states both. A flat administrative fee and a daily interest rate can apply to the same overdue invoice — just add them separately rather than compounding one into the other.

**Is this compound or simple interest?**
Simple interest, calculated daily against the original invoice amount — it does not compound, meaning interest never accrues on interest that's already built up.
